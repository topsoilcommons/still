This is a machine back translation check, not the fluent human check the file still awaits. Method: each Spanish string was back translated into English from the Spanish alone, and that back translation was then compared against the English source in en.json. A string is marked disputed when the back translation shows meaning shifted, softened, hardened, made clinical where the English is plain, or anything added or dropped, a placeholder broken, or a fact altered. ## Reading level: Fernandez Huerta

Score: 75.00

Formula as specified: 206.84 minus 0.60 times syllables per 100 words minus 1.02 times sentences per 100 words.

Inputs, counted over all 60 translated content strings (locale, status, note excluded):

- Sentences per 100 words: 13.61
- Calculation: 206.84 minus 117.96 minus 13.88 equals 75.00

Band: 75.00 falls in the 70 to 80 band, usually labelled fairly easy (algo fácil) on the Fernandez Huerta scale, where 80 to 90 is easy and 90 to 100 is very easy. Common school level mappings put the 70 to 80 band at roughly seventh grade, but that mapping is a convention of the tables, not part of the formula, and is stated here only as a rough guide.

Counting method, stated honestly. Syllables were counted with a script written for this check (/tmp/count_fh.py), not by hand and not with a dictionary. The script counts vowel nuclei in each word: strong vowels (a, e, o) next to another strong vowel count separately (hiatus); a written accented weak vowel (í, ú) breaks a diphthong and counts separately; other weak plus strong, strong plus weak, and weak plus weak combinations count as one nucleus, and weak plus strong plus weak as one triphthong. Silent u in que, qui, gue, gui is removed first; h between vowels is ignored; a standalone y counts as one syllable. The script was sanity checked on 18 known words (including herramienta 4, dispositivo 5, habitación 4, saborear 4, fría 2, oír 2) and matched on all 18. Words are alphabetic tokens only. Pure numerals (988, 741741, 4, 6, the 5-4-3-2-1 sequence) and the placeholders {n} and {time} are excluded from the word and syllable counts, because the syllable count of a numeral depends on how a reader says it. Sentences are counted as terminal punctuation marks (full stop, question mark, exclamation mark) followed by a space or the end of a string; the dot inside 988lifeline.org is not followed by a space and is not counted. Known weaknesses of this method: names kept in English (Still, Aurora Commons, Ascent, Crisis Text Line, Behavioral Health Partners, HOME, and others) are counted with Spanish vowel rules, which will mis-count some of them; and heading and label fragments with no terminal punctuation count as zero sentences, which lowers the sentences per 100 words figure and pushes the score up, so 75.00 is if anything a generous reading of how easy this copy is.

Comparison to the English baseline. BASELINE.md records the English page at Flesch-Kincaid grade 2.5, with Flesch reading ease 90. Fernandez Huerta is the Spanish adaptation of that same reading ease scale, so the ease scores are comparable: English 90 against Spanish 75.00 is a drop of about 15 points and a drop of one full band, from very easy to fairly easy. On the rough school level mappings, that is about grade 2.5 for the English against roughly grade 7 for the Spanish. Part of the gap is structural, since Spanish words carry more syllables, and the 0.60 coefficient is the formula correction for exactly that, so the remaining gap should not be waved away as just how Spanish is. Long abstract words such as herramienta, dispositivo, respiración, and confidencial do real work in raising the count. Conclusion: the Spanish copy reads noticeably harder than the English source, and it does not yet meet the reading level the English page was measured at.

## The translator flagged hard strings: this check on each

1. Numb (checkin.words.Numb, "Insensible"). Disputed. See the table. Insensible reads as insensitive or unfeeling toward others, or physically without sensation, and as a self description label it is clinical and mildly accusatory where the English is a plain neutral state. The rendering in checkin.lines.Numb, "No sentir nada", is acceptable and agrees, which makes the label the inconsistent part.
2. Okay (checkin.words.Okay and checkin.lines.Okay, "Bien"). Disputed. Bien is good or well, a positive state. Okay is the neutral middle the page goes out of its way to legitimise ("Okay counts", "Okay is a real place to be"), and in Spanish that middle option is simply missing from the list a visitor picks from. Any replacement belongs to the fluent checker, but the check records that Bien does not carry the English meaning.
3. Check in wording (checkin.heading, checkin.done, header.tagline, metaDescription). Disputed. There is no rendering of the term at all: the heading becomes a question ("Cómo te sientes"), the confirmation becomes a generic "Ya está", and the tagline and meta description paraphrase it as "ver cómo estás". Each paraphrase is understandable on its own, but together the section loses its name, its confirmation loses its referent, and a Spanish reader gets no single term for the first of the page three tools, while the other two tools keep theirs (Respira, Escribe). That inconsistency is the core problem.
4. Grounding heading (grounding.heading). Disputed. The term grounding is dropped rather than translated or kept, and the heading becomes a purpose paraphrase that duplicates grounding.sub. A reader is told what the practice does but never what it is called, so they cannot recognise it elsewhere or look it up.
5. Hopeful line (checkin.lines.Hopeful). Disputed. Softened and shifted in three places, detailed in the table: protecting becomes looking after, the person state becomes the abstract noun hope, and "Future you" becomes a generic future. The line still lands as warm, but it is not the same line, and "lo que esperas" adds an expect or wait ambiguity the English does not have.
6. Crisis Counselor title (crisis.line2Text). Disputed as staged. Leaving the title in English may turn out to match the Spanish usage of the service itself, and only a fluent checker with those Spanish materials can settle that. Until then it stands as an untranslated key term in a crisis line, opaque to the very reader this file exists for, and plain Spanish words for the role exist. The surrounding restructure, "un voluntario con formación", also weakens "trained" and is part of the dispute.

## Bottom line

47 of 60 content strings agree cleanly, including all five grounding steps, all crisis facts and numbers, both placeholders, and the whole Write section. That pattern, plus a Fernandez Huerta score of 75.00 against an English reading ease of 90, is what the fluent human check should work on first.
